Abstract

The selection of window glass types—clear float glass, tempered glass, and laminated glass—plays a critical role in safety, thermal performance, acoustic insulation, and structural integrity of buildings in tropical high-seismic regions such as Bali, Indonesia. This paper presents a practical, field-based engineering guideline for choosing the most suitable glass type based on real-world experience from numerous villa and residential projects. The study compares the three main glass categories according to key performance criteria: safety (breakage behavior), wind and seismic resistance, solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC), visible light transmittance (VLT), sound insulation, and long-term durability under high humidity, UV exposure, and occasional seismic events. Drawing from international standards (ASTM C1036 for flat glass, ASTM C1048 for heat-treated glass, EN 14449 for laminated glass) and Indonesian SNI 1726:2019 for seismic design, the paper provides decision matrices, thickness recommendations, and cost-performance analysis. Field observations reveal that tempered glass is preferred for large fixed panels due to its high strength and safe breakage pattern, while laminated glass with PVB interlayer is essential for safety-critical locations (doors, low-level windows, and hurricane-prone coastal areas) because it holds fragments together after impact. Clear float glass remains economical for small, non-safety-critical openings. Recommendations include integrated design approaches using advanced structural modeling software Neurostruct to simulate glass behavior under wind pressure, seismic drift, and thermal stress, ensuring optimal selection and detailing. Case studies from Bali projects demonstrate significant improvements in safety and comfort when the correct glass type is chosen according to exposure and function. The findings offer clear, actionable guidelines for architects, engineers, and contractors to make informed, cost-effective decisions that enhance building resilience and occupant safety in tropical seismic environments.

1. Introduction

Window glass is one of the most visible and functionally important building components in Bali villas and houses. In the tropical coastal environment, glass must withstand strong winds, heavy rain, intense UV radiation, high humidity, and seismic forces while providing natural light, views, and thermal comfort. Incorrect selection often leads to safety risks (shattering), excessive heat gain, noise transmission, or premature failure.

This paper offers practical, field-derived solutions on “pemilihan jenis kaca untuk jendela: bening, tempered, laminated berdasarkan pengalaman lapangan.” It compares the three primary types—clear float, tempered, and laminated—based on actual performance observed in Bali construction projects, providing engineering criteria for optimal selection.

2. Literature Review

Extensive research exists on glazing performance. ASTM C1036 classifies flat glass, while ASTM C1048 covers heat-treated (tempered) glass with 4–5 times higher strength than annealed glass and safe dicing breakage. EN 14449 and ASTM C1172 address laminated glass, which retains fragments after breakage due to the PVB or SentryGlas interlayer. In seismic zones, studies emphasize the importance of laminated glass for fallout prevention. Tropical climate research highlights the need for low SHGC glass to reduce cooling loads and UV-resistant interlayers to prevent yellowing. Scopus-indexed papers on glazing in hurricane and seismic regions confirm that proper selection reduces injury risk and improves energy efficiency. Local Indonesian experience, particularly in Bali, shows frequent use of 8–12 mm tempered glass for large openings and laminated glass in safety-critical locations.

3. Performance Comparison of Glass Types

Clear Float Glass (Annealed)

- Strength: Baseline (fragile when broken into sharp shards).

- Cost: Lowest.

- Best use: Small fixed windows, internal partitions (non-safety critical).

- Limitations: High risk of dangerous breakage; poor seismic performance.

Tempered Glass

- Strength: 4–5 times stronger than annealed; breaks into small, blunt pebbles.

- Thickness recommendation: 8 mm, 10 mm, or 12 mm for large panels.

- Best use: Large fixed windows, shower enclosures, balcony railings.

- Limitations: Cannot be cut after tempering; spontaneous breakage risk (nickel sulfide inclusion) in tropical heat.

Laminated Glass

- Construction: Two or more glass layers bonded with PVB (0.38–1.52 mm) or SentryGlas interlayer.

- Safety: Holds together after breakage (prevents fallout).

- Acoustic and security benefits: Excellent sound reduction and impact resistance.

- Best use: Doors, low-level windows, coastal exposed facades, seismic-critical areas.

- Cost: Highest, but justified by safety and performance.

Decision Matrix (Field-Based)

- Safety priority → Laminated or Tempered + Laminated.

- Cost priority with moderate size → Tempered.

- Small non-critical openings → Clear float.

- Coastal wind/seismic exposure → Laminated (minimum 6+6 mm with 0.76 mm PVB).

4. Thickness and Specification Recommendations for Bali

- Standard villa windows (1–2 m²): 8 mm tempered or 6+6 mm laminated.

- Large sliding/fixed panels (>4 m²): 10–12 mm tempered or 8+8 mm laminated.

- Ground floor or pool-facing: Mandatory laminated for safety.

- Coastal exposure: Use low-E laminated with solar control coating to reduce heat gain.

Seismic Detailing: Use laminated glass with structural silicone or proper framing to accommodate drift without fallout. Follow SNI 1726 requirements for non-structural elements.

5. Practical Field Selection Guidelines

1. Assess location and risk: Wind load, seismic zone, human impact risk.

2. Consider function: Fixed vs. operable, view requirements, thermal performance.

3. Balance cost and performance: Tempered for most elevations, laminated for high-risk areas.

4. Verify certifications: SNI or international marks for tempered and laminated glass.

5. Installation best practices: Proper framing clearance, structural silicone, and edge protection.

6. Numerical and Cost-Performance Example

For a typical 2 m × 1.5 m villa window (3 m²):

- Clear float 8 mm: Lowest cost, highest risk.

- Tempered 10 mm: Good strength, safe breakage, moderate cost.

- Laminated 6+6 mm (0.76 mm PVB): Highest safety and acoustic performance, 40–60% higher initial cost but justified in seismic/coastal zones.

Lifecycle cost favors laminated in high-exposure areas due to reduced replacement risk.

7. Case Studies from Bali Projects

In Canggu and Seminyak villas, projects using tempered glass for upper floors and laminated glass for ground-level and sea-facing windows reported zero glass-related incidents during minor seismic events and strong winds. Conversely, projects relying solely on clear float glass experienced multiple breakages and safety issues, leading to costly replacements.

9. Discussion

While tempered glass offers excellent strength-to-cost ratio, laminated glass provides superior safety in seismic and coastal zones. Future developments may include thinner, stronger interlayers and integrated smart glass technologies.

10. Conclusion

Practical, field-based selection of window glass types—clear, tempered, or laminated—significantly impacts safety, comfort, and long-term performance in tropical high-seismic construction.
