Abstract (English Version)

Small-scale construction projects, particularly individual and medium-sized villas in Bali, require formwork solutions that balance cost, simplicity, speed, and quality without the heavy investment typical of large modular systems. This paper examines efficient and cost-effective formwork systems for reinforced concrete beams in small-scale construction projects in full compliance with Indonesian National Standard SNI 2847:2019 (Persyaratan Beton Struktural untuk Bangunan Gedung), which modifies ACI 318M-14 provisions. Supporting standards include SNI 1727:2020 for minimum design loads and SNI 1726:2019 for earthquake-resistant design.

The study compares conventional timber/plywood beam formwork with semi-system and lightweight modular aluminum systems, focusing on practical adaptations suitable for limited site access, smaller crews, and irregular beam layouts common in Bali villas. Key topics include lateral pressure management, simple shoring and reshoring strategies, joint sealing for leak prevention, chamfer detailing for neat edges, alignment methods, concrete placement techniques, and curing protocols under tropical conditions.

1. Introduction

Small-scale construction projects in Bali, such as private villas, boutique hotels, and residential houses, represent a significant portion of the local building industry. These projects typically involve limited budgets, smaller workforces, constrained site access, and irregular architectural layouts. Beam formwork in such contexts must be simple to erect, economical, and still deliver acceptable surface quality and structural safety. SNI 2847:2019 Section 26.11 requires formwork to be rigid and tight enough to prevent mortar loss while supporting proper reinforcement placement for seismic performance per SNI 1726:2019.

This paper provides a rigorous yet practical analysis in the English segment for broader engineering audiences and a field-oriented Indonesian segment tailored for local contractors, foremen, and small-project engineers working on Bali villa constructions.

2. Literature Review and Code Framework

SNI 2847:2019 governs structural concrete and indirectly controls beam formwork through requirements for concrete quality, cover, and consolidation. ACI 347 Guide to Formwork for Concrete offers practical recommendations for smaller projects. Field observations in Bali show that many small-scale projects still rely on conventional timber formwork due to its low initial cost and flexibility for irregular beam shapes. However, semi-system and lightweight aluminum solutions are gaining popularity as they reduce labor and improve finish quality without requiring large capital investment. In humid coastal Bali, proper formwork execution is critical to avoid plastic shrinkage cracks and ensure durability of beams that are often partially exposed.

3. Lateral Pressure Calculations for Small-Scale Beam Formwork

Even in small projects, understanding lateral pressure helps prevent form bulging and grout leakage.

Simplified lateral pressure formula for beam side forms (adapted from ACI 347):

p = C_w × C_c × [150 + 9000 × R / (T + 18)] (kPa)

Where:

- C_w = 1.0 (normal weight concrete)

- C_c = 1.0 (Type I cement)

- R = rate of placement (m/h)

- T = concrete temperature (°C)

For a typical villa beam with side height 0.55 m, R = 1.2 m/h (small crew placement rate), T = 30°C (Bali condition):

p ≈ 150 + 9000 × 1.2 / (30 + 18) = 150 + 10800 / 48 ≈ 150 + 225 = 375 kPa

p_max limited by height (150 × 0.55 = 82.5 kPa) or practical system capacity. Use conservative spacing of ties and additional bracing for safety on small sites.

4. Practical Formwork Systems Suitable for Small-Scale Projects

# 4.1 Conventional Timber/Plywood Beam Formwork

Widely used in small Bali villa projects due to low cost, availability of local materials, and flexibility for non-standard beam shapes. Requires careful bracing and sealing.

# 4.2 Semi-System and Lightweight Modular Solutions (Recommended Transition)

- Hybrid systems using aluminum or steel secondary beams with plywood soffit or lightweight panels.

- Offer better reusability (50–150 cycles) and improved surface quality compared to fully conventional methods.

- Easier handling on small sites with limited crane access.

Field-Friendly Techniques for Small Projects:

- Standardize beam widths where possible to reduce custom cutting.

- Use simple adjustable steel props or bamboo/timber shoring combined with modern secondary beams.

- Apply chamfer strips for neat edges on visible beams.

- Employ small-team erection sequences that do not require heavy equipment.

5. On-Site Execution Sequence for Small-Scale Villa Projects

1. Preparation: Cut and assemble materials on site; apply release agent.

2. Shoring Installation: Place props at safe spacing based on beam span and load.

3. Soffit and Side Assembly: Install bottom form, then side panels; secure with ties or nails.

4. Alignment: Use spirit level and string lines for straightness (small projects often lack laser tools).

5. Inspection: Check joints, cover spacers, and cleanliness before pouring.

6. Concreting: Pour in one continuous operation with manual vibration.

7. Stripping and Curing: Remove forms after 24–48 hours; apply moist curing or curing compound for at least 7 days.

6. Quality Control, Safety, and Common Challenges in Bali Small Projects

Focus on basic but effective quality checks: cover measurement, joint tightness, and vibration adequacy. Safety measures include stable shoring and proper access. Common challenges in Bali small sites include limited space, manual labor dependency, and rapid drying—addressed by timely curing and wind protection.
