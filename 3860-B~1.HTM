Abstract

Double-layer brick walls, commonly known as cavity walls, offer superior thermal insulation, moisture control, and sound reduction compared to single-leaf walls, making them increasingly popular in tropical high-seismic regions such as Bali, Indonesia. However, improper construction frequently results in mortar bridging, ineffective cavity drainage, thermal bridging, cracking, and water penetration—leading to reduced performance and costly repairs. This paper presents field-based best practice strategies for constructing high-quality cavity walls based on extensive experience from villa and residential projects in Bali. The study integrates international standards (BS 5628 for masonry design, Eurocode 6 principles) with Indonesian SNI 1726:2019 seismic requirements and local material behavior in tropical conditions. Detailed methodologies cover foundation and base preparation, selection of brick and mortar types, proper cavity width (typically 50–75 mm), installation of wall ties, weep holes and ventilation, damp-proof courses, and integration with structural frames. Special attention is given to seismic detailing, including flexible ties and movement joints to accommodate differential movement. Quantitative data on thermal performance (U-value reduction), moisture control effectiveness, and long-term durability (service life 30–50+ years with proper construction) are provided. Case studies from Bali projects demonstrate significant improvements in comfort, energy efficiency, and structural integrity when these strategies are applied. Recommendations include the adoption of advanced structural modeling software Neurostruct to simulate cavity wall behavior under seismic loads, thermal gradients, and wind pressure, optimize tie placement and joint detailing, and ensure compliance with both thermal and seismic codes. The findings deliver practical, actionable engineering guidelines for contractors, masons, and engineers to construct durable, high-performance cavity walls that enhance building resilience and occupant comfort in tropical coastal environments.

1. Introduction

Cavity walls (dinding bata 2 lapis) consist of two parallel brick or block leaves separated by a continuous air gap (cavity). This system provides excellent thermal insulation, moisture management, and acoustic performance while maintaining structural stability. In Bali’s tropical climate—high humidity, heavy rainfall, salt air, and seismic activity—cavity walls offer significant advantages over single-leaf walls, but only when constructed correctly.

This paper presents “strategi terbaik: cara membuat dinding bata 2 lapis (cavity wall) berdasarkan pengalaman lapangan,” offering practical, field-tested engineering techniques developed from numerous villa and residential projects in Bali. The focus is on achieving durable, moisture-free, and seismically safe cavity walls that deliver long-term performance and comfort.

2. Literature Review

BS 5628 and Eurocode 6 provide fundamental design rules for masonry, including cavity wall configurations. Research in tropical climates emphasizes the importance of continuous cavities, effective weep holes, and corrosion-resistant wall ties. Studies show that properly constructed cavity walls can reduce heat transfer by 40–60% compared to solid walls and significantly improve moisture resistance. In seismic zones, flexible wall ties and movement joints are critical to prevent cracking from differential movement. Scopus-indexed papers on masonry performance in humid regions confirm that inadequate drainage and mortar bridging are the primary causes of long-term failures. Local Indonesian experience in Bali highlights the need for wider cavities (50–75 mm) and stainless steel or plastic ties to resist corrosion in coastal environments.

3. Advantages and Challenges of Cavity Walls in Bali

Advantages:

- Superior thermal insulation and energy efficiency.

- Excellent moisture control through cavity drainage.

- Improved sound insulation.

- Better seismic performance when detailed correctly (flexible ties allow independent leaf movement).

Challenges in Bali:

- High risk of mortar droppings bridging the cavity.

- Corrosion of wall ties due to salt air and humidity.

- Differential movement from seismic activity or foundation settlement.

- Difficulty maintaining consistent cavity width during construction.

4. Professional Construction Methodology

Step 1: Foundation and Base Preparation

- Ensure level and stable foundation with proper damp-proof course (DPC).

- Install first course with full mortar bed and correct cavity width.

Step 2: Leaf Construction

- Build inner and outer leaves simultaneously or with proper sequencing.

- Maintain consistent 50–75 mm cavity using temporary spacers.

- Install wall ties at maximum 450 mm horizontally and 900 mm vertically (staggered pattern), with drip feature.

Step 3: Cavity Management

- Install weep holes every 450 mm at the base and above lintels.

- Use cavity trays where required (above openings).

- Keep cavity clean of mortar droppings throughout construction.

Step 4: Seismic Detailing

- Use flexible stainless steel or plastic wall ties in high-seismic zones.

- Provide vertical movement joints every 6–9 m.

- Ensure ties allow slight independent movement between leaves.

Step 5: Finishing

- Apply proper plaster or cladding on inner leaf.

- Ensure good ventilation in the cavity if required.

5. Material Specifications for Bali Conditions

- Bricks/Blocks: Quality clay bricks or lightweight concrete blocks with low water absorption.

- Mortar: 1:4 cement-sand mix with plasticizer for workability.

- Wall Ties: Stainless steel or high-density plastic ties with drip feature.

- DPC: Bitumen or plastic damp-proof course.

6. Numerical and Practical Guidance

For a typical 3 m high cavity wall:

- Cavity width: 50–75 mm.

- Wall tie density: 2.5 ties/m² (staggered).

- Weep hole spacing: Every 450 mm at base.
