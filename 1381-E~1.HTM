2. Literature Review

International standards (BS EN 1935 and ANSI/BHMA) classify hinges according to duty grade, cycle testing, and corrosion resistance. Research published in *Construction and Building Materials* and *Journal of Performance of Constructed Facilities* indicates that screw holding capacity in wood is highly dependent on edge distance, screw length, pilot hole size, and wood species density.

In tropical climates, corrosion of hinge screws and loosening due to cyclic moisture movement are major concerns. Studies recommend stainless steel or corrosion-resistant hinges and proper pilot hole drilling to maximize holding power. Ball-bearing hinges are preferred for heavy doors to reduce friction and wear.

3. Challenges in Tropical Environments

Common challenges include:

- Wood movement due to moisture content changes causing hinge misalignment.

- Reduced screw holding capacity in humid conditions.

- Corrosion of mild steel hinges and screws in coastal areas.

- Overloading from heavy solid wood doors.

- Vibration and slamming forces in frequently used doors.

- Poor edge distance leading to splitting of wooden frames or doors.

4. Engineering Principles for Strong Hinge Installation

# 4.1 Hinge Selection

- Use heavy-duty ball-bearing hinges for doors > 40 kg.

- Grade 304 or 316 stainless steel for coastal Bali areas.

- Minimum 3 hinges for standard doors, 4 hinges for heavy or tall doors.

# 4.2 Positioning and Spacing

- Top hinge: 150–200 mm from top of door.

- Bottom hinge: 200–250 mm from bottom.

- Middle hinge centered or slightly above for even load distribution.

# 4.3 Fixing Technique

- Pre-drill pilot holes (70–80% of screw diameter).

- Use full-threaded screws of adequate length (minimum 2.5–3 times door thickness).

- Apply wood glue or epoxy in pilot holes for enhanced holding in humid conditions.
