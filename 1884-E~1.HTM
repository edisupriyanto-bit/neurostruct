Abstract

Communal septic tanks serve as critical decentralized wastewater treatment infrastructure for residential clusters, commercial developments, and tourism facilities in areas lacking centralized sewerage systems. Common failure modes—cracking, leakage, reduced anaerobic digestion efficiency, and groundwater contamination—are addressed through reinforced design, quality materials, and systematic quality control. 2. Literature Review

#2.1 Design Principles and Standards

Septic tanks function primarily through sedimentation and anaerobic digestion. Key references include:

- EPA Onsite Wastewater Treatment and Disposal Systems Design Manual (1980/2002 updates).

- WHO Guidelines for On-Site Sanitation.

- ISO 24521 and ISO 24525 for operation and maintenance of on-site domestic wastewater services.

- Indonesia SNI 2398:2017 for blackwater disposal systems.

Design parameters emphasize sufficient volume for hydraulic retention time (HRT), sludge/scum storage, and quiescent flow conditions to promote solids separation and partial BOD reduction (typically 30–50% in the tank).

For communal systems serving multiple households or buildings (e.g., 10–100 users), multi-compartment designs (2–3 chambers) with baffles are recommended to enhance treatment efficiency and prevent short-circuiting.

#2.2 Volume Calculation and Sizing

Wastewater flow (Q) is estimated as:

Q = n × q × f_c

Where n = number of users, q = specific water consumption (L/capita/day, often 100–150 in tropical residential settings), f_c = correction factor.

Liquid volume (V_L) ≈ Q_p × t_HRT, with peak flow considerations and HRT typically 24–48 hours.

Sludge and scum volume depends on accumulation rates, desludging period (t), and sizing factor (f_s) influenced by temperature (higher temperatures in Bali accelerate digestion, allowing slightly smaller factors).

Total tank volume V = V_L + V_s + V_sc.

Typical liquid depth: 1.2–2.2 m; length-to-width ratio: 2–4:1.

#2.3 Tropical and Seismic Considerations in Bali

High ambient temperatures (>20°C) enhance anaerobic activity but increase corrosion risks and evaporation effects. Clay soils common in parts of Bali reduce percolation rates, necessitating larger soakaway fields or advanced treatment (e.g., constructed wetlands). Seismic design requires reinforced concrete with proper detailing per SNI seismic codes to prevent cracking.

Literature highlights frequent failures due to undersizing, poor watertightness, inadequate desludging, and groundwater intrusion in coastal or high-rainfall zones.

4. Results and Discussion

Properly sized communal septic tanks with 24–48 hour HRT achieve effective solids removal and partial organic stabilization. In tropical conditions, higher temperatures support faster digestion, but require robust corrosion protection (e.g., sulfate-resistant cement or coatings). Multi-compartment designs reduce effluent solids by 20–30% compared to single-chamber units.

Common issues in Bali include leakage from poor joint sealing or seismic cracking, leading to groundwater contamination. Engineered solutions—such as waterstops at construction joints, minimum concrete cover, and reinforcement detailing—mitigate these risks. Soakaway or absorption field design must account for local percolation rates; in low-permeability clay soils, mounded systems or wetlands are recommended.

Neurostruct enhances outcomes through phased quality checkpoints, ensuring compliance with standards and maximizing service life (10–20+ years with regular desludging).

Cost-benefit: Initial engineering investment increases construction cost by 10–15% but reduces long-term maintenance, desludging frequency, and environmental remediation expenses by 30–50%.

5. Recommendations and Professional Tips for Maximum Results

1. Conduct thorough site assessment including soil percolation tests and groundwater level monitoring.

2. Size tank conservatively using the equations above, with at least 2–3 compartments for communal use.

3. Use high-quality reinforced concrete (minimum M25 grade) with proper waterproofing admixtures and waterstops.

4. Ensure minimum liquid depth 1.2 m and adequate freeboard for scum/sludge.

5. Install baffles or tees to maintain quiescent conditions and prevent short-circuiting.

6. Provide robust access covers and ventilation; locate tank downhill from water sources (minimum 15–30 m separation).

7. Integrate with appropriate effluent disposal (soakaway, wetland, or advanced treatment) suited to local soil and rainfall.

8. Schedule desludging every 2–5 years based on monitoring; implement community management for communal systems.

9. Incorporate seismic detailing per local codes for Bali.
